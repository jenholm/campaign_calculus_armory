# Historical Case Review v2 (M71)

## Franco-Prussian War (1870-71)

### Current Framing
"The decisive shock merely completed a process of exhaustion that was already well underway."

### Problem
This framing is too reductive. It risks being a straw man.

### Revised Framing
Sedan was decisive AND prior attrition mattered. These are not contradictory.

- **Decisive element**: The Battle of Sedan captured Napoleon III and destroyed the main French field army. This was a genuinely decisive operational event.
- **Exhaustion substrate**: France had already suffered significant manpower depletion, economic strain, and territorial losses before Sedan.
- **Revised language**: "Sedan converted accumulated strategic disadvantage into political collapse."

### Key Point
The Franco-Prussian War is the paradigmatic case of the attritional iceberg: the visible decisive shock operated atop an attritional substrate. The shock was real; the exhaustion was also real. Both mattered.

## Vietnam War (1965-75)

### Current Framing
Classified as "strategic exhaustion" with political collapse as terminal event.

### Problem
The current framing misses the complexity of how exhaustion actually manifested.

### Revised Framing
Move from binary to "strategic exhaustion with decisive accelerators":

- **Tet Offensive (1968)**: A decisive shock that accelerated political exhaustion but did not cause it. The US was not militarily defeated at Tet, but the political impact was devastating.
- **Political exhaustion**: Two decades of cumulative attrition eroded American political will. Tet was the accelerant, not the fire.
- **Final offensive (1975)**: North Vietnam's final offensive exploited exhausted South Vietnamese defenses. The terminal event was military collapse, but the mechanism was exhaustion.
- **Revised language**: "Strategic exhaustion with decisive accelerators: cumulative attrition eroded political will, while specific shocks (Tet, final offensive) accelerated but did not cause the underlying trajectory."

## World War II (1939-45)

### Current Framing
Classified as "strategic exhaustion" with mixed dynamics.

### Problem
The reviewer correctly notes that WWII involved both exhaustion and decisive events.

### Revised Framing
"Strategic exhaustion with decisive accelerators":

- **Exhaustion substrate**: Industrial mismatch, manpower depletion, fuel collapse, multi-front pressure. Germany and Japan could not replace losses.
- **Decisive accelerators**: Barbarossa failure (1941), Midway (1942), Normandy (1944), atomic bombs (1945). These events accelerated defeat but did not cause it.
- **Key distinction**: The Axis was already losing before each decisive event. The events accelerated an exhaustion-driven trajectory.
- **Revised language**: "Exhaustion with decisive accelerators: industrial mismatch and manpower depletion created the conditions for defeat; specific operational events accelerated the timeline."

## World War I (1914-18)

### Current Framing
Classified as "strategic exhaustion."

### Assessment
The classification is broadly correct. The Western Front was an industrial attrition system.

- **Exhaustion substrate**: Trench warfare, economic blockade, manpower depletion.
- **Decisive accelerators**: Hundred Days Offensive (1918) was an operational breakthrough atop exhaustion substrate.
- **Revised language**: "Strategic exhaustion: the Western Front was an industrial attrition system where the economic blockade and cumulative manpower losses determined the outcome. The Hundred Days was an operational exploitation of exhausted German capacity."

## Summary

The key revision is moving from binary "exhaustion" labels to more nuanced "exhaustion with decisive accelerators" where appropriate. This acknowledges that:
1. Exhaustion creates the conditions for defeat
2. Decisive events accelerate but do not cause the underlying trajectory
3. Both mechanisms operate simultaneously at different phases
